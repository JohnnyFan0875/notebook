# Course Creation Workflow

## Why Course Creation Needs a Workflow

Instructional content is not just writing slides or exercises. A course has to move through design, development, review, launch, and maintenance with clear ownership and predictable handoffs.

Without an explicit workflow, common failure modes include:

- missed deadlines
- inconsistent learner experience
- weak alignment between objectives and exercises
- technical debt after launch

## High-Level Lifecycle

A practical course lifecycle can be viewed as three broad phases:

| Phase | Main question |
| --- | --- |
| Design | What should the learner be able to do by the end? |
| Development | How do we build lessons, exercises, narration, and review assets? |
| Maintenance | How do we keep the course accurate and effective after release? |

## Start with Learning Objectives

Good course design begins with clear objectives, not with slide production.

Useful objectives specify:

- what the learner should understand or perform
- the expected level of difficulty
- what deliverables or assessments will prove learning

Objectives act as the contract between curriculum design, content development, and review.

## Timeline and Accountability

A course timeline is valuable because it creates:

- accountability on both sides
- transparency in the production process
- predictability for launch planning

A realistic plan should include not only writing time, but also:

- review cycles
- recording or screencast preparation
- technical setbacks
- schedule interruptions such as travel or other work

The main lesson is that optimistic plans fail unless they account for revision and real-world interruptions.

## Stakeholders

Course creation usually serves more than one audience at once.

Common stakeholders include:

- the instructor or author
- learners
- internal curriculum or content teams
- business stakeholders who care about adoption, retention, or customer value

A good workflow makes these stakeholders visible early instead of discovering conflicting expectations at launch time.

## Development Deliverables

During development, course work is typically split into multiple asset types:

- chapter outlines
- lesson scripts
- exercises and solutions
- hints and success messages
- screencasts or narration
- review notes and revisions

This matters because "the course" is really a bundle of synchronized artifacts.

## Pedagogical Quality Checks

Before launch, a strong course should be checked for:

- objective-to-exercise alignment
- clarity of instructions
- pacing and cognitive load
- awkward wording in spoken or written explanations
- consistency between examples, code, and expected answers

Reading a script silently is not enough. Saying explanations out loud or walking through exercises end to end often reveals friction that is invisible in draft form.

## Review and Pre-Launch Validation

Useful pre-launch artifacts often include:

- screencasts or rehearsal recordings
- full-course walkthroughs
- exercise correctness checks
- issue tracking for unclear instructions or broken code paths

The key idea is that review should happen on the learner experience, not just on isolated content fragments.

## Soft Launch vs Hard Launch

A staged release is often safer than treating launch as one single event.

| Stage | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Soft launch | Expose the course to real learners, gather feedback, and validate quality signals |
| Hard launch | Promote the course more broadly once quality and performance are acceptable |

This reduces the risk of heavily promoting a course before its rough edges are visible.

## Post-Launch Maintenance

Course quality decays unless maintenance is part of the workflow.

Recurring maintenance usually includes:

- checking learner feedback
- reviewing completion and satisfaction metrics
- fixing confusing prompts, hints, or code
- updating outdated content or dependencies

Maintenance is not an optional afterthought. It is part of the content lifecycle.

## Ownership of Fixes

One useful practice is to separate what the author owns from what platform or tooling teams own.

For example, authors often own:

- instructional context
- wording of instructions and hints
- example code
- pedagogical clarity

Meanwhile, platform teams may own:

- validation infrastructure
- test harnesses
- rendering or delivery systems

Clear ownership reduces review churn and avoids ambiguous "someone should fix this" states.

## Practical Takeaways

- Start from learning objectives, not from slide production.
- Build timelines that include revision, review, and real interruptions.
- Treat course content as a coordinated set of artifacts, not a single document.
- Use staged launches and feedback loops to improve quality before broad promotion.
- Plan maintenance from day one if the content is meant to stay useful.
